## Business Problem

A mid-size services firm kept being blindsided by resignations. People who left described long-standing frustrations leadership had never heard. The official channels — surveys, open-door policies, skip-levels — existed, but nobody used them honestly until they were already halfway out the door.

## Psychological Explanation

This is a **Confidence** problem: low psychological safety. When admitting a mistake or naming a problem feels risky, people withhold. The silence is not apathy; it is self-protection. Weak **Connection** compounds it — without a trusted manager, there is no safe first place to raise a concern.

## Organisational Consequence

Leadership operated on a flattering but false picture. Small, cheap-to-fix issues compounded into expensive, structural ones. By the time feedback arrived, it arrived as a resignation letter — the most costly and least actionable form of feedback there is.

## Behavioural Economics Angle

Silence is rational under **loss aversion**. The personal downside of speaking up — being seen as difficult, blamed, or sidelined — is concrete, immediate, and lands on you. The upside — a maybe-improvement, shared across everyone — is diffuse and delayed. A rational employee weighs a vivid private cost against a vague public benefit and stays quiet. You cannot exhort people out of a sound cost-benefit calculation; you have to change the payoffs.

## Recommended Intervention

- Make it visibly safe to raise concerns and admit mistakes — leaders model it first by owning their own.
- Close the loop: show people the actions taken because they spoke up, so the upside becomes concrete.
- Separate "raising a problem" from "being the problem" in how managers respond.

## Measurable Outcome

After a focused psychological-safety effort, the audit's Confidence score rose into the Watch band and, more tellingly, issues began surfacing in 1:1s rather than exit interviews. Problems got cheaper to fix because they arrived earlier.
