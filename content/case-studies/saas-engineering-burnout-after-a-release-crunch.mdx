## Business Problem

A 90-person SaaS company had shipped a major release after a nine-week crunch, then lost two senior engineers within a month. Leadership read it as bad luck. Delivery was slowing, on-call was getting heavier, and the people who had been the team's strongest performers looked flat — but with no hard evidence, the problem kept losing the budget argument to the roadmap.

## Psychological Explanation

An anonymous 5C audit (36 responses) put numbers on it. The overall Workplace Mind Score landed in the **Risk** band, but the shape mattered more than the headline: **Capacity** had fallen into the **Critical** band, and **Connection** sat in **Risk**. Under the Job Demands–Resources model, that pairing — sustained demand with no recovery, and managers too stretched to buffer it — is the classic burnout signature. The audit's combined-risk rule flagged it automatically: low Capacity plus low Connection is high burnout risk.

## Organisational Consequence

The department comparison localised the damage. Engineering scored materially below Sales and Customer Success, and was the segment where risk concentrated. Against sector and size peers, the company sat in the **bottom quartile for Capacity and Connection** — not a vague "we're stressed," but a defensible position relative to comparable SaaS firms. Smaller teams were suppressed from the report entirely, so no individual could be inferred.

## Behavioural Economics Angle

The crunch had quietly rewritten the incentives. Visible intensity during the release had been praised; recovery had not. That made overwork a **status signal**, and people rationally supply more of whatever earns status — so the most committed engineers depleted fastest. The two departures were not random; they were the predictable output of an incentive the company had built by accident and kept rewarding.

## Recommended Intervention

The audit fed a phased 90-day plan rather than a generic wellbeing push:

- **Stabilise (days 1–30):** take immediate load off Engineering — cut, automate, or resequence the lowest-value work, and set explicit recovery norms (protected focus time, no routine after-hours contact).
- **Rebuild (days 31–60):** coach the new engineering managers on supportive 1:1s and workload triage; re-establish the manager cadence the departures had broken.
- **Embed & re-measure (days 61–90):** turn the fixes into standing norms and re-run the 5C audit against the baseline.

## Measurable Outcome

The closed audit→cost loop made the stakes concrete: the generic burnout estimate of roughly £1.46m rose to a modelled **£1.59m** once the company's actual 5C scores were applied, concentrated in Engineering — a defensible figure produced by the company's own data, framed throughout as a modelled estimate, not a guarantee. With a named cost, a named cause, and a named team, the intervention finally won the budget it had been losing for two quarters.
