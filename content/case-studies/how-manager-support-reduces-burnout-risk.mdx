## Business Problem

Two teams in the same company, with near-identical workloads, reported wildly different wellbeing. One was stable and productive; the other churned. The work was not the variable. The manager was.

## Psychological Explanation

In the Job Demands–Resources model, **Connection** — manager support, recognition, fair handling of conflict — is a *resource* that buffers the strain of high demands. The same workload feels survivable when someone has your back and unbearable when you are carrying it alone. High **Capacity** demand is tolerable only when Connection is there to offset it.

## Organisational Consequence

The unsupported team's strain leaked into everything downstream: more sick days, slower delivery, and turnover that pulled knowledge out of the team and raised the load on those who stayed. The supported team, under the same demands, kept compounding.

## Behavioural Economics Angle

Manager support works like a reliable **subsidy** on the cost of staying. Each act of timely support lowers the personal price of a hard week; over time those small, dependable subsidies shift the cost-benefit of staying versus leaving. Crucially, reliability beats grand gestures — an occasional wellbeing perk is a lottery ticket; a manager who consistently has your back is a dependable return, and people weight dependable returns far more heavily.

## Recommended Intervention

- Train managers in the few high-leverage skills: supportive 1:1s, workload triage, and constructive conflict handling.
- Protect managers' own capacity so they can actually do the people work, rather than treating it as overhead.
- Build specific, regular recognition into team rhythms.

## Measurable Outcome

After targeted manager training, the weaker team's Connection score moved up two bands and its Capacity strain eased — without any change to the underlying workload. The lever was never the work. It was who people answered to.
